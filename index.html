Changemakers Course Guide Draft

Changemakers is a Grade 10 course at Nido de Aguilas that combines social studies, art, design thinking, research, communication, collaboration, creativity, adaptive thinking, and action. The course asks students to make meaningful change in a community, but it does not treat action as a quick final product. Students are expected to listen, research, revise, communicate with a real audience, and use feedback to make better decisions.

The course is co-taught. The materials assume approximately 80-minute blocks and four sections with different teaching pairs. That matters because the course depends heavily on shared daily slides, Schoology postings, and teacher judgment during work time.

Assessment Backbone

The course uses five competencies.

  • Creativity: Students generate, iterate, and create useful or novel work.
  • Collaboration: Students contribute, communicate, respond to feedback, and handle team dynamics.
  • Adaptive Thinking: Students use evidence, systems thinking, source evaluation, and multiple perspectives.
  • Communication: Students shape messages for an audience using the conventions of a medium.
  • Action & Advocacy: Students work with stakeholders, propose or implement change, and analyze impact.

L2 assessments are usually guided checkpoints. They make sure essential process moves happened. L3 assessments ask students to demonstrate independent proficiency with evidence. L4 assessments look for synthesis, stakeholder integration, intentional craft, sustained impact, or metacognitive depth.

How the Course Actually Runs

The course lives in daily slide decks. Students often use the slides as the pacing document, instruction sheet, and reference point for what was done in class. Schoology holds assignments, submission points, links, and the week-by-week course shell. Trello was used or intended as a project management space, especially for complex project work.

A new teacher should expect the course record to be split across places. In practice, the slides usually tell the story of what happened in class. Schoology tells what students were asked to submit. The rubrics tell what the team was trying to assess. The unit guide files in this archive pull those pieces together.

Projects Students Completed

Students first practiced changemaking through small, bounded experiences. They interviewed a classmate and made something tangible for them. They completed a nuclear semiotics design sprint where they proposed ways to communicate danger far into the future.

In Unit 1, students studied how consumer products affect people across a lifecycle. Each student researched one perspective, such as a factory worker, farmer, consumer, shipping worker, marketer, or waste worker. Students used CRAAP source evaluation, database research, AI-supported summaries, and individual research analysis. The final product was an interactive graphic made from a Photoshop collage and Genially hotspots.

In Unit 2, students learned documentary film language. They practiced shot types, framing, editing, sound, storyboarding, interview work, and visual storytelling. The project moved from a small object film into a documentary connected to curiosity, local issues, advocacy, and audience change.

In Unit 2.5, students returned to documentary work after break. They audited footage, research, and story. They selected roles, revised narrative arcs, added missing B-roll or research, and prepared a final film and presentation.

In Unit 3, students formed teams around broad interests, narrowed topics, contacted stakeholders, conducted empathy interviews, researched the issue, proposed an action, built prototypes, collected feedback, implemented or tested the work, and presented evidence of change.

What New Teachers Need To Watch For

Students often move too quickly to a solution. The strongest parts of the course slow them down around empathy, research, stakeholder perspective, evidence, and problem definition.

Students can produce polished media that hides weak thinking. Rubrics and checkpoints need to ask for research, revision, collaboration, and stakeholder feedback, not just final products.

Group work needs evidence. The course should not assume equal contribution because a team produced one artifact. Collaboration surveys, role notes, meeting evidence, and teacher observations matter.

The course needs a clear home base. In 2025-2026, the slides, Trello, Schoology, verbal instructions, and section-specific decisions were all in play. That gave teachers flexibility, but it also made the course hard to follow.
