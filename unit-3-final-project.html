Unit 3: Final Changemaker Project

Purpose

The final project asked students to move from structured projects into more authentic community action. Students worked in teams around shared interests, found stakeholders, built empathy, researched the issue, proposed an action, prototyped, collected feedback, implemented or tested the work, and presented evidence of impact.

What Students Did

Students started with broad interest teams, then narrowed topics. The slide deck uses examples such as people with disabilities, animal shelters, art, ocean waste, software development, fast fashion, nutrition, music, community building, and human trafficking.

Students identified stakeholders and contacted them by email. The stakeholder letter framed the partnership as a request for one to three meetings, possible written feedback, and a final survey. Students were asked to copy teachers on communication.

Students prepared empathy interviews through knowledge audits and question planning. They researched stakeholder organizations and used the research to write better questions. They then moved toward proposals, prototypes, stakeholder feedback, implementation, thank-you notes, and final presentations.

Teaching Sequence

SequenceWhat HappenedWhy It Mattered
Team launchStudents started in broad interest teams, made team agreements, named what helps and hurts group work, and created rules for communication, quality, deadlines, accountability, and follow-up.The unit asked for more independence than earlier projects. The team agreements were a first checkpoint for whether groups could manage that independence.
Topic narrowingTeams moved from broad interests to a narrower topic. The slides used macro, meso, and micro lenses so students could separate systems, community conditions, and individual experiences.This kept students from treating a broad concern as a project. A team interested in disability, for example, needed to decide which part of disability access they could actually understand and affect.
Stakeholder searchTeams generated at least six possible stakeholders, researched contact information, emailed potential partners, copied teachers, and responded professionally when people replied or declined.The project depended on people outside the classroom. The slides made stakeholder communication an early deadline rather than something students could postpone until they had already designed a solution.
AI design sprintStudents used Gemini Canvas to experience the difference between a generic tool built without empathy and a tool built after asking what someone wanted. They then built a small app, tool, game, or site for a partner and iterated on it.This was an empathy lesson disguised as a making activity. Students felt what happens when designers skip the person they are designing for.
Empathy interview preparationStudents researched stakeholders or organizations, completed knowledge audits, wrote interview questions from what they knew and wanted to know, and scheduled interviews.Students could not move forward responsibly without knowing the stakeholder's needs. Research was used to write better questions, not just to collect facts.
Post-interview sensemakingTeams divided Canvas cards for interview questions, post-interview reflection, stakeholder research, core problem, root causes, Five Whys, and consequences.This gave students a place to turn interview information into project decisions. It also helped teachers see which teams were still guessing.
L2 Adaptive Thinking researchStudents summarized key points from sources, connected those points to project planning, and received feedback about making evidence more specific.This checkpoint prepared students for the L3 proposal. It pushed them away from general statements like "music helps people" and toward evidence that could actually support a decision.
Define and ideateTeams defined the stakeholder's problem with a sentence frame, researched what had already been tried, brainstormed student-sized ways to make change, generated bad ideas, turned some bad ideas into possible ones, and mapped ideas on a How, Now, Wow chart.This was the main anti-rushing scaffold. Students could not jump from "we care about this" to "we know the solution." They had to define, research, generate, sort, and choose.
Vision, change, and SMART goalTeams wrote a vision, named the change they wanted to create, wrote SMART goals, identified a minimum viable project, listed community support, planned for resilience, and considered sustainability and scalability.The project had to become specific enough to execute. These cards turned a hopeful idea into a plan with audience, action, constraints, helpers, timeline, and backup plans.
L3 proposalStudents wrote an individual proposal email to the stakeholder and explained how research, stakeholder feedback, and multiple perspectives supported the proposed action. Teams then compared individual emails and sent the best combined version.The assessment doubled as real project work. Students had to communicate professionally and justify the action before asking the stakeholder for feedback.
Rough prototypeStudents made a fast prototype by the end of class, sent it to the stakeholder, and asked for feedback. The slides emphasized that a prototype could be a sketch, mockup, app, lesson outline, event outline, model, website mock, or storyboard.This prevented teams from staying in planning mode. The goal was to put an early version in front of someone and learn from it.
Refined prototype and Red TeamStudents revised toward a more useful prototype, sent the refined version to stakeholders, and used a Red Team pre-mortem to find failure modes related to effort, burnout, handoff, resources, logistics, funding, and long-term survival.This shifted the question from "Is this a good idea?" to "Can this survive contact with the real world?"
Implementation and testingTeams moved toward small-scale pilots, dry runs, user interviews, A/B tests, CAR pitches, and implementation while continuing to follow up with stakeholders.Students were told not to wait passively for replies. If a stakeholder was slow, they still needed to test a smaller version or prepare the next action.
Communication and promotionSome lessons focused on SMART goal review, target audience, pain points, features versus benefits, reducing friction, social proof, and urgency.Projects that needed participation or behavior change needed communication strategy. Students had to think about why someone would actually use, attend, read, or care.
Final presentationStudents prepared a 3-5 minute individual presentation with visuals. They had to explain the before and after, how research and stakeholder feedback shaped the change, successes, failures, deeper issue understanding, multiple perspectives, and sustainability.The final presentation was assessment evidence, not just a recap. Students had to show Action & Advocacy, Adaptive Thinking, and Communication in one coherent explanation.
Practice and reflectionStudents practiced hooks, pauses, eye contact, movement, vocal variation, visual design, peer feedback, collaboration surveys, thank-you notes, final presentations, success gallery, failure reflection, and advice for next year.The unit ended by treating presentation craft, collaboration, gratitude, failure, and future advice as part of the changemaking process.

Scaffolding

The unit used several structures to keep open-ended work from becoming vague:

  • Team agreements with communication, work quality, deadlines, accountability, and follow-up.
  • Macro, meso, and micro lenses for understanding issues.
  • Stakeholder lists and outreach deadlines.
  • Knowledge audits before interviews.
  • A proposal email that required students to connect action to research and stakeholder feedback.
  • Prototype deadlines and stakeholder feedback requests.
  • Red Team pre-mortem for failure modes.
  • Canvas checklist for vision, stakeholders, root causes, action plan, obstacles, partners, and sustainability.
  • Final presentation requirements focused on before-and-after change, stakeholder feedback, research, successes, failures, and learning.

The biggest scaffold was the Canvas. It gave teams a place to record empathy work, define the problem, brainstorm evidence, build a proposal, plan a prototype, track feedback, plan for resilience, and show individual contributions through initials.

The unit also used deadlines as scaffolds. Students had to have a stakeholder, conduct an interview, complete Canvas cards, send a proposal, send a prototype, send a refined prototype, and complete or test the project by specific dates. The deadlines were not just administrative. They kept open-ended work from drifting.

The design process was broken into decision points. Students had to decide the stakeholder, the problem, the evidence, the possible actions, the best action, the prototype, the test, the implementation plan, and the final explanation. Each decision had a document, Canvas card, or assessment attached to it.

The final presentation was built from content and craft. Students had requirements for what to explain, but they also practiced how to present: hooks, pauses, eye contact, movement, visuals, organization, timing, and peer feedback.

What A New Teacher Should Understand

This unit is the clearest version of the course's intended release of responsibility. Students have more choice, but the work still needs deadlines, evidence, and teacher checkpoints.

The hardest part is stakeholder dependency. Students cannot wait passively for replies. The slides repeatedly tell students to keep researching, follow up, and test small-scale versions while they wait.

The final presentation is not just a celebration. It is assessment evidence. Students need to show the change they made, how they decided on that change, what feedback and research shaped it, what worked, what failed, and how they understand the issue more deeply now.
