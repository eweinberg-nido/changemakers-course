Unit 2: Documentary Film

Purpose

This unit moved students from still-image communication into documentary film. Students learned how filmmakers use curiosity, story, shots, interviews, B-roll, editing, sound, and audience awareness to make meaning.

What Students Did

The unit began with smaller film exercises and moved toward a larger documentary project.

Students explored visual storytelling through a 45-second documentary and object film work. They practiced storyboarding, rule of thirds, shot variety, camera stability, environmental context, cutaways, and editing. The object film asked students to create curiosity around an ordinary object through framing, camera angle, movement, editing, and sound.

The larger documentary work asked students to identify a topic, conduct research, interview people, gather footage, and shape a film around a message. Students were asked to think about what they wanted the audience to do or believe after watching the film.

Teaching Sequence

SequenceWhat HappenedWhy It Mattered
Documentary launchStudents watched documentary examples and discussed why an audience cares about a person or story. They looked at shot types, interview framing, and lighting before making anything larger.The first move was not "pick a topic." It was "notice how film creates interest." Students needed a language for looking before they could plan.
Lighting and shot practiceStudents practiced close-up, medium, and wide shots, then used reflectors or lights to compare available lighting with Rembrandt-style lighting. They added comparisons to a class slide deck.This made film craft physical. Students could see that moving a light, phone, or subject changed the feeling of an interview.
45-second documentaryStudents shared stories about someone who made a change in their life, wrote a short story, passed it to a partner, storyboarded three shots, filmed in a controlled setting, and edited an individual short film.This was a short production cycle. Students practiced story, shot planning, cutaways, rule of thirds, sound, landscape orientation, editing, export, and upload before the larger team film.
Community curiosity and DocathonStudents brainstormed people, facts, observations, curiosities, and frustrations about Nido, Santiago, and Lo Barnechea. They used affinity mapping to find possible areas of interest and connected the work to the Docathon theme, Bridging the Gap.This kept the documentary local. Students were pushed away from broad topics like "climate change" and toward a filmable person, place, issue, or community question.
The Object challengeStudents made a one-minute film with no dialogue, at least ten shots, steady camera, and a focus on creating curiosity around an object. They storyboarded before filming.The object film isolated film language from social issue complexity. It asked students to use framing, sequence, pacing, angle, and editing to make an ordinary object interesting.
Project systemsTeams set up Trello, a shared folder, collaboration feedback from Project 1, personal collaboration goals, working agreements, and a place to document ideas.The documentary involved interviews, footage, research, deadlines, and role responsibilities. The systems were meant to prevent the project from becoming a pile of loose files and verbal agreements.
Idea developmentTeams used an idea generator, an Iceberg analysis, and purpose questions to test whether an idea had enough depth. The slides explicitly separated feasible ideas from huge or generic topics.The project needed a filmable story. The Iceberg model helped students look beneath the first topic idea and ask what causes, assumptions, or hidden systems might be involved.
Interview preparationStudents wrote interview questions, studied what makes a strong interview question, practiced active listening and follow-up questions, and rehearsed by interviewing an AI version of their topic.Interviews were not treated as a simple checklist. Students needed open-ended, respectful, specific questions that could lead to real stories and useful footage.
Background researchTeams divided research, gathered basic context, followed up with interviewees, and used the first interview to identify new research needs.Research was meant to support the film rather than sit in a separate document. Students needed enough context to ask better questions and interpret what interviewees said.
Production planningStudents built team calendars and agendas, planned B-roll, checked sound recording, cleaned audio with Adobe Podcast, gathered footage, and started assembling the documentary.The slides repeatedly named who was behind and what needed to be finished. That mattered because documentary production has many small dependencies.
Rough cut and final pushStudents worked toward a rough cut, completed a collaboration survey, reviewed requirements and rubrics, and submitted the film by the deadline. Late or incomplete films had clear consequences tied to SCR and collaboration grade caps.The deadline structure made production real. Students had to move from planning and gathering footage into editing decisions.

Scaffolding

The unit taught film in layers.

First, students learned to see. Shot size, interview framing, lighting, and rule of thirds were taught before students were responsible for a full documentary. The lighting exercise and 45-second documentary made those skills concrete.

Second, students practiced short form filmmaking. The 45-second documentary and object film were not side activities. They were rehearsals for the final film: plan shots, film deliberately, edit, export, and submit.

Third, students narrowed broad curiosity into filmable subjects. The Docathon brainstorms, affinity mapping, Iceberg analysis, and feasibility discussions helped students move from a topic to a story.

Fourth, students prepared for interviews. The course slowed down around question-writing, active listening, and follow-up questions because the documentary depended on real voices, not just student narration.

Fifth, students made process evidence part of the work. Trello, shared folders, calendars, agendas, B-roll plans, research notes, rough cuts, and collaboration surveys gave teachers ways to see what happened before the final film.

What A New Teacher Should Understand

The documentary unit depends on process evidence. Students need to show interviews, footage, story decisions, research, and revision. A polished final film alone is not enough evidence.

The roles matter. Director, editor, and cinematographer give students different ways to show creativity, but teachers need evidence from each role. Without role evidence, individual grading becomes hard.

Students may confuse a topic with a story. The strongest scaffolds ask: Who is the main character? What do they want? What is in the way? What changes by the end?
