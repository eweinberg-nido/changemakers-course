Unit 1: Journey Of A Product

Purpose

Students investigated how personal consumer choices affect other people across the lifecycle of a product. The unit combined research, perspective taking, Photoshop collage, and interactive media. The final product was an interactive graphic in Genially built from a Photoshop collage and hotspots that communicated research about one lifecycle perspective.

Driving Question

How do the personal decisions that I make impact others?

Final Product

Each team selected a consumer product. Each student researched a different perspective from the product lifecycle, such as harvesting, manufacturing, branding, shipping, consumption, or disposal. Each student created a Photoshop collage connected to the team visual style, then built an interactive graphic with hotspots that showed research-based information about their perspective.

The intended final product included:

  • A Photoshop collage that used symbolic imagery and a shared artist style.
  • A Genially interactive graphic with hotspots.
  • At least five pieces of hotspot information.
  • Credible and linked sources.
  • A message about the selected perspective.
  • Evidence that visuals, text, and research worked together.

All student interactive graphics from the unit are collected in the Student Interactive Graphics Showcase.

Teaching Sequence

SequenceWhat HappenedWhy It Mattered
Team setup and working agreementsStudents moved into project teams, gave collaboration feedback from the nuclear semiotics sprint, wrote working agreements, used scenarios about uneven participation, and set up Trello.The project was too complex to rely on "just work together." We asked teams to name how they would divide work, communicate, and handle problems before the project got stressful.
Art as changeStudents looked at examples like the AIDS Memorial Quilt, Fearless Girl, and documentary or photographic examples of public impact.This helped students see the final product as more than decoration. The collage and interactive graphic were supposed to communicate a researched point of view.
Family interviewsStudents interviewed family members about recent purchases, uploaded transcripts or audio, summarized the findings, and compared patterns across families. Some teams used AI to look for additional commonalities across interview transcripts.This connected the driving question to real choices at home. It also gave students an early empathy move before the unit shifted into broader product lifecycle research.
Product choice and perspective mappingTeams chose a consumer product, checked whether it had enough research potential, used AI as a brainstorming assistant for lifecycle perspectives, and selected individual perspectives to investigate.Students needed a product broad enough to research but specific enough to make. The perspective map prevented the group from all researching the same thing.
Source credibility and CRAAPStudents practiced source evaluation with obvious and less obvious examples, including dhmo.org, worldhistory.org, government reports, blogs, journals, and persuasive sources. They then completed the L2 CRAAP assessment.The course did not assume students could already judge sources. The lessons separated disagreement from credibility and pushed students to name strengths and limitations.
Database and source-finding practiceStudents learned where to find academic sources, how to use database search terms, when to start with Google, and how to expand or shift keywords when sources were hard to find.Many students got stuck because their exact product or perspective did not produce easy search results. The slides gave fallback strategies: broaden keywords, look for related industries, use reputable news, and return to CRAAP when leaving databases.
Shared research challengeTeams used a shared document to find an article, create an MLA citation, summarize it, name a strength and limitation, and select quotes tied to their perspective. Peers then gave one affirmation and one suggestion before revision.This was a rehearsal for the individual L3 research assessment. It made common problems visible: weak MLA, weak limitations, vague summaries, and evidence that did not really connect to the perspective.
Individual L3 researchStudents completed individual research using three sources. The model used bakery worker research to show source credibility, quadrant alignment, AI summary, direct evidence, and student-added insight.This is where Adaptive Thinking became assessable. AI could help summarize, but students had to show what the summary missed and explain why the missing information mattered.
Problem statement and hotspot planningStudents turned research into a problem statement: a specific group needs a way to do something because of a researched problem. They then selected the most important information for the L2 Communication interactive graphic planning document.This was the bridge from research to message. Students were asked to stop collecting facts and decide what the viewer needed to understand.
Visual research and sketchingStudents organized team perspectives, named what each perspective cared about, studied visual coherence, brainstormed symbolic images, selected an artist style, made thumbnails, received team feedback, and revised sketches.The art scaffolding made the Photoshop piece serve the message. Teams had to think about individual perspective and shared visual style at the same time.
Photoshop foundationStudents learned or reviewed pixel versus vector art, image downloading, resolution, layers, selections, masks, adjustment layers, and non-destructive editing. The hybrid animal task gave them a bounded way to practice before the final collage.Students had uneven Photoshop experience. The hybrid animal task separated tool practice from the higher-stakes communication task.
Final collage and Genially buildStudents chose orientation as a team, downloaded high-resolution images, built final collages, placed hotspots in Genially, revised hotspot detail, checked visual consistency with teammates, and prepared final submissions.The final stretch repeatedly returned to team coherence and audience clarity. Students were not just finishing their own image. They were building a set of related interactive graphics.

Scaffolding

The scaffolding moved from people, to research, to message, to image.

The first move was personal. Family interviews made consumption specific before students entered the full lifecycle of a product. Students could talk about why a family bought something, what they considered, and what they ignored.

The second move was structural. Perspective mapping gave each student a role inside the lifecycle. Harvesting, manufacturing, production, shipping, consumption, and disposal each led to different research questions and different human consequences.

The third move was research quality. CRAAP practice, database work, shared research documents, peer review, and the L3 model all prepared students to evaluate sources and make claims from evidence. The course used AI, but the assessment looked for student judgment: what source is credible, what viewpoint is represented, what evidence matters, and what the AI summary missed.

The fourth move was communication. Problem statements and hotspot planning forced students to decide what they were trying to say. This is where many students needed teacher support, because facts did not automatically become a message.

The final move was craft. Students practiced Photoshop separately before using it for the final product. Sketching, artist style, team visual coherence, image quality, masks, adjustment layers, and Genially hotspot placement all served the same goal: make research understandable to a viewer.

What A New Teacher Should Understand

The unit asks students to hold several kinds of work at once: research, empathy, product lifecycle thinking, Photoshop technique, visual symbolism, and interactive design. Students need a clear minimum path through the unit. Without that, they can spend time moving between tools without knowing what decision they are making.

The hardest instructional move is connecting research to message. Students can collect facts and make attractive collages without forming a clear argument about the perspective. The hotspot planning document and final rubric are meant to force that connection, but they need careful timing.

The AI research workflow needs explicit boundaries. Students need to know which parts AI can support and which parts must show their own thinking.
