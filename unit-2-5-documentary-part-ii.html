Unit 2.5: Documentary Part II

Purpose

This phase returned to documentary work after break and pushed students from collected footage toward a clearer final film. The emphasis was revision: audit the story, audit the B-roll, audit the research, then make decisions that improve the documentary.

What Students Did

Students selected or clarified production roles:

  • Director: narrative arc, protagonist, critical quotes, story clarity.
  • Editor: transitions, sound integration, pacing, contrast, narrative economy.
  • Cinematographer: B-roll, composition, lighting, visual evidence, technical quality.

They audited existing work:

  • Story audit: What story is being told? Who is the main character? What do they want? What obstacle do they face? What is the resolution?
  • B-roll audit: What footage exists? What is missing? What footage does not support the story?
  • Research audit: What research informs the narrative? What questions still need to be answered?

Students then wrote a proposal statement that connected subject, conflict, goal, and strategy. The handouts pushed students to identify the hook, the want, the obstacle, the action, and the resolution.

Scaffolding

The audit structure is the most important scaffold. It turns a vague instruction like "make your film better" into decisions students can act on:

  • Find anchor quotes.
  • Identify missing B-roll.
  • Identify research that supports the story.
  • Decide what the audience should understand or do.
  • Make the role-specific work easy to see.

The other important scaffold is the story test. Students repeatedly asked whether the film had a main character, a want, an obstacle, and a resolution. When those pieces were missing, the team had to decide whether to change the story, gather more footage, or make the filmmakers' investigation the story.

The role structure also mattered. A director could not simply say "the story is unclear." They had to identify anchor quotes and narrative shape. A cinematographer could not simply say "we need more footage." They had to name missing visual evidence. An editor could not simply assemble clips. They had to use pacing, sound, and research to make the film easier to understand.

What A New Teacher Should Understand

This phase is a good model for revision in the course. Students often need help seeing that revision is not decoration. It is a process of making stronger decisions based on evidence.

The most useful teacher questions are practical:

  • What is the film actually about right now?
  • Where do we see the evidence?
  • What is missing that would help the audience believe or care?
  • What does each role need to do before the next checkpoint?
