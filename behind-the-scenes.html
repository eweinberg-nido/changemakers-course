Behind The Scenes Notes

This file is intentionally private-facing. It collects issues, tensions, and reorganization questions that should inform future course design but should not sit inside the public-facing course guide.

Main Input

The seed document for these notes was a private teacher problem list for the Journey of a Product unit. Additional issues were inferred from the file structure, Schoology sequence, and unit materials.

Too Many Places To Look

The course used several operational spaces at once: daily slides, Schoology, Trello, verbal instructions, Google files, and section-specific teacher decisions. This gave teachers flexibility, but it made it difficult for students and teachers to know exactly where to look.

Reorganization question: Where should students and teachers look first for daily tasks, deadlines, and current project status?

Possible direction: Use one public student-facing hub per unit. The daily slides can still pace class, but the hub should hold the stable task list, assessments, project checkpoints, and links.

Unit 1 Planning And Rubric Timing

The L2 Communication interactive graphic planning document appears to have been collected before students fully knew what their final collage and hotspots would become. That created a mismatch between the rubric and the actual stage of student thinking.

Reorganization question: Should the planning document be moved later, after the collage concept is clearer, or split into an early rough plan and a later assessed plan?

Possible direction: Treat the early version as formative planning only. Assess the revised version after students have enough visual direction and research depth to make meaningful hotspot choices.

Research And AI Boundaries

The Unit 1 research workflow intentionally used AI for summaries and support. The problem list notes that some students used AI for much more than the intended parts. Some students processed AI output in ways that showed thinking, while others did not.

Reorganization question: How should the course distinguish AI-supported work from student thinking in a way students can actually follow?

Possible direction: Label every task field as AI allowed, AI optional, or student thinking only. Require students to highlight or otherwise mark their own additions to AI-generated summaries. Assess the student's critique, source connection, and added insight rather than the AI summary itself.

Research Slog

The product lifecycle unit made inquiry and research central, but the problem list names a real issue: research can become a slog, especially when students are more engaged by making, designing, filming, and building.

Reorganization question: Can students learn research in context rather than completing too much research before making anything?

Possible direction: Alternate making and research more deliberately. For example, have students produce a rough visual or prototype earlier, then use research to revise it. This would make research a tool for improving work rather than a gate before the engaging part starts.

Art And Social Studies Integration

The course is half art and half social studies. Unit 1 shows the tension clearly. The art component is engaging and skill-based. The inquiry component is essential to the course mission but can feel disconnected if students do not see why the research matters.

Reorganization question: What must every project prove as art, and what must every project prove as social inquiry?

Possible direction: Write each unit with two non-negotiables. One should name the medium-specific craft evidence. The other should name the evidence that students understand people, systems, or stakeholders.

Message Versus Exposure

The problem list raises a fundamental disagreement: Do all students need to form a clear opinion or argument by the end of a unit, or is exposure to complexity sometimes enough?

Reorganization question: Which projects are argument projects, and which projects are complexity or exposure projects?

Possible direction: Be explicit. Unit 1 appears to require a message or argument in the interactive graphic. If that remains true, build toward it from the beginning. If some projects are meant to focus on exposure and understanding, assess them differently.

Interactive Graphic Hotspot Requirement

There was uncertainty about whether the central message should be one of the five hotspots, placed elsewhere, or treated as a sixth hotspot.

Reorganization question: Where does the message live in the final interactive graphic?

Possible direction: Require a central message panel plus five evidence hotspots. That avoids forcing the message to compete with evidence and makes the argument clear to the viewer.

Four Sections, Four Versions

The course ran across four sections with different teaching pairs. Decisions made in one section did not always transfer to another. Teachers had limited time to debrief and coordinate between blocks.

Reorganization question: What minimum decisions must be consistent across all sections, and what can vary by section?

Possible direction: Create a same-day section log with three fields: what we completed, what changed, what students need next class. Keep it short enough that teachers will actually use it.

Assessment Design Pressure

The problem list notes that rubrics were sometimes built while the unit was already moving, and that changes to the unit did not always lead to changes in the rubric.

Reorganization question: What assessments need to be finished before the unit starts?

Possible direction: For each unit, finalize the L3 anchor rubric first. Then design L2 checkpoints backward from that rubric. Keep L4 as a qualitative extension rather than a list of extra tasks.

Student Variability

Students struggled for different reasons: missing skills, weak effort, confusion, group dynamics, AI misuse, and uneven readiness. The team did not always have a shared response for what to do when students missed checkpoints.

Reorganization question: What happens when students do not meet a checkpoint, and what happens when they are ready to move ahead?

Possible direction: Build a checkpoint response menu for each major project. It should include remediation, required revision, extension, and teacher conference options.

Good Elements To Preserve

  • Daily slides make the lesson path easy to follow.
  • The course has a strong defined hybrid path when the checkpoints are clear.
  • The competency scales give the assessments a shared structure.
  • The Make Someone's Day task is a strong early empathy experience.
  • The Unit 1 perspective structure gives students a way into systems thinking.
  • The documentary audit structure gives students a practical way to revise.
  • Unit 3 stakeholder work is the strongest connection to authentic Action & Advocacy.
  • The final project presentation asks for real evidence: before and after, stakeholder feedback, research, success, failure, and learning.

High-Value Reorganization Tasks

1. Decide where students and teachers should look first for student-facing tasks. 2. Build one stable unit hub per unit. 3. Create a project map that shows phases, artifacts, assessments, and competencies. 4. Finish L3 rubrics before each unit begins. 5. Mark AI use expectations directly in each student document. 6. Separate formative planning from assessed planning. 7. Add a same-day section log for co-teaching coordination. 8. Require stakeholder and research evidence before final products are assessed.
