Unit 0: Start Of Year And Nuclear Semiotics

Purpose

This opening sequence introduced students to Changemakers as a course about meaningful change, not generic service or leadership. Students built community, defined what a changemaker is, practiced empathy through a small peer-based challenge, and completed a short design sprint around nuclear semiotics.

What Students Did

Students began by meeting the teaching team and learning the course frame. The course was presented as half art and half social studies, with assessment categories connected to creativity, communication, collaboration, critical thinking or adaptive thinking, and action & advocacy.

Students discussed whether they already saw themselves as changemakers. They compared examples of changemakers, created a scale for "more" or "less" changemaker, and talked about the criteria they used to judge impact.

The first empathy activity was Make Someone's Day. Students interviewed a classmate, planned a tangible surprise for that person, and carried it out without buying anything. The point was to make empathy practical. Students had to use what they learned from another person rather than guessing what would be appreciated.

The nuclear semiotics mini-project introduced design thinking and creative problem solving. Students considered the problem of warning people about nuclear waste far into the future, generated and revised ideas, used feedback, and presented a solution.

Assessments And Evidence

The nuclear semiotics project used two L2 Creativity assessments:

  • Creative product: students produced a useful idea or solution for the problem.
  • Creative process: students generated, iterated, and explored ideas through teacher-directed tools and protocols.

Students also generated collaboration evidence through team design work and oral presentation evidence through the final share.

Teacher Notes

This opening sequence matters because students practice empathy before the larger projects begin. The Make Someone's Day task is small enough to complete quickly, but it makes the course's core point clear: good design starts with listening.

The nuclear semiotics sprint gives students an early experience with ambiguity, constraints, feedback, and revision. It also gives teachers a chance to name the difference between a clever idea and an idea that responds to a real problem.

The sequence also introduces three habits that return all year: use a process, keep evidence of thinking, and explain why a decision fits the audience and problem.
